## 1. Photo catalog — Craig's 4 Showa street references

### Photo 1 — shotengai storefront (real photo, likely 1970s–80s preserved streetscape)
A wooden two-story machiya storefront, the most information-dense of the four:
- **Materials:** vertical plank timber facade (lower half), white plaster upper, dark kawara tile roof with visible ridge tiles, wooden lattice window frames.
- **Signage system (the star):** hand-painted wooden fascia boards (酒 ビール, 酒 洋食 丼物 うどん), white noren curtain with bold black kanji (御食事), hanging vertical menu banners (さば寿し, 親子丼, うどん, 一品料理, ひやビール — each a narrow cloth strip), red nobori-style vertical banner at left, small enamel/printed plates by the door, menu posters in the windows (geisha portraits, Kirin beer girl).
- **Street furniture:** white mamachari bicycle with basket, potted palm, wooden sandwich board, wall-mounted lamp, visible power meter box.
- **Layering law:** signage exists at 4 depths — fascia (top), noren (doorway), banners (window plane), plates (hand height). The street reads as *information sediment*.

### Photo 2 — bonnet bus + Showa museum (real photos, top and bottom)
- **Top:** 1950s bonnet bus (Isuzu-style: exposed engine hood, round headlights, cream/red two-tone, roof destination blind, split windshield) parked on a shotengai street. Globe streetlamps (yellow/green globes), shop awnings, noren, shoppers in modern clothes — the bus is the anachronism, the street is the continuity.
- **Bottom:** Showa museum interior — 1964 Tokyo Olympics memorabilia (flags, posters, ticket stubs), kei truck (Mazda K360-style), Tetsujin-28 and Astro Boy figures, model planes. The *curated* Showa: what the era chose to remember about itself.
- **Takeaway:** the bonnet bus is the era's vehicle signature — pre-cab-over, truck-chassis, honest mechanical. The museum half says: this civilization *archives* its optimism.

### Photo 3 — anime still (tram street, Japanese-European blend)
From an Osaka Momoyo promo (the "In This Corner of the World" school of historical anime — meticulous period reconstruction):
- **The blend:** left building is European — arched windows, pilasters, stone quoins, mansard-ish roof with dormers; right buildings are Japanese — kawara tile roofs, timber frames, plaster walls. They share the street without comment.
- **Transit signature:** red tram (single car, clerestory roof) on embedded rails, and above everything the **overhead wire jungle** — catenary spans, feeder poles, a forest of copper.
- **Ground:** stone pavers/setts, not asphalt. A kimono-clad woman crosses — the street belongs to feet and trams, not cars.

### Photo 4 — anime still (Showa policeman, dusk streetscape)
Same historical-anime school:
- **The policeman:** dark wool uniform, peaked cap with badge, mustache, white gloves — the *koban* system's human face. Authority as a fixture, not a threat.
- **The crowd:** kimono, haori, fedoras, workman's caps — mixed dress, all on foot.
- **Streetscape:** wooden machiya with lattice (koshi) windows glowing from within, hanging kanban signboards perpendicular to the street (the original *vertical* signage), noren, a handcart (daihachiguruma). Dusk light, warm windows — the street as living room.

## 2. Film research — Ghibli's WWII-era Japan and its neighbors

### The Wind Rises (Ghibli, Miyazaki, 2013) — the primary text
Miyazaki's own look notes for the film (from the production booklet): paint Taisho-to-early-Showa Japan beautifully; don't hide architecture in sepia — be "bold with East Asian colors of modernism"; roads bumpy and uneven; "shop signs and billboards line up chaotically"; "jumbles of wooden utility poles are everywhere." The film's industrial Japan is defined by **catching up**: grass runways, oxen dragging aircraft from factory to airfield, Mitsubishi's Nagoya works as a cathedral of rivets. Key steal for Kurogane Bay: the *earnestness of industry* — machines are loved, maintained, hand-tended. Our dust-era mechanics inherit this: carburetors tuned by ear, vacuum tubes babied, machines as relationships.

### From Up on Poppy Hill (Ghibli, 2011) — 1963 Yokohama
The port city at its most romantic: foreign-settlement slopes, the Latin Quarter's cluttered clubhouse, harbor trams. Steal: Yokohama as the *harbor city with a European slope* — directly feeds Nagisa/Daikoku's foreign-settlement DNA.

### Grave of the Fireflies (Ghibli/Takahata, 1988) — wartime Kobe
Kobe's burning streets, the air-raid shelters, the burned-out tram. Steal: the *texture of loss* — charred timber, melted glass, the city as something that survives by scraps. Our dust is slower than fire but the same teacher: civilization as salvage.

### In This Corner of the World (MAPPA, 2016 — not Ghibli, but Craig's photo 3/4 school) — Kure/Hiroshima 1944–45
The gold standard of period street reconstruction in anime: every noren, every wire, every tram stop drawn from photo archives. Steal: the *method* — build from photo references, never from memory. (This is already our standard 3D workflow.)

### The common thread
All four films treat the street as **a machine for living that people maintain by hand**. Nobody in these films is alienated from their infrastructure — they sweep it, oil it, argue with it. Kurogane Bay's dust-era citizens inherit this: they don't live *in spite of* the dust, they *tend* the city against it.

## 3. Real-world anchors

- **Tram cities that survived:** Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Kagoshima, Toyohashi, Kochi — Japan's remaining streetcar networks. The tram is the Showa transit signature: single-car, clerestory roof, brass fittings, the *chin-chin* bell. In Kurogane Bay the tram survives as the Harbor Tram (already in the bible) — now explicitly a *dust-armored* descendant.
- **Bonnet buses:** 1950s Isuzu TX / Nissan / Toyota — truck chassis + bus body, exposed hood, round headlights, destination blind. Built for bad roads; perfect dust-era ancestor.
- **Enamel signage:** horo kanban — vitreous enamel on steel, the pre-neon advertising surface. Fades but never rots; sand-blasting *improves* its legibility by removing grime. Ideal dust-era survivor.
- **Noren culture:** the split curtain as shop identity, season marker, and dust barrier. Already a dust flap in waiting.
- **Shotengai arcades:** the covered shopping street — Japan's pre-mall high street. In the dust era, the arcade roof becomes a *survival structure*: the only walkable commercial space is under cover.

## 4. The crazy Tokyo architecture projects (the upward extension)

- **Metabolism (1960s):** skeleton-vs-cells — permanent infrastructure megastructures with transient plug-in living units. Nakagin Capsule Tower (Kurokawa, 1972): 140 capsules bolted to 2 concrete shafts, meant to be replaced every 25 years (never were). The *plug-in city* is our capsule-tower housing law.
- **Kenzo Tange's Tokyo Bay Plan (1960):** an 18 km linear megastructure *across Tokyo Bay* — three levels of looping highways, an "arterial structure" for a city of 10 million, designed around the automobile as the shaper of space. Never built. For Kurogane Bay: the bay was *supposed* to get Tange's megastructure; what got built instead was cheaper, dustier, stranger — the floating panel fields are the Bay Plan's ghost.
- **Expo '70 (Osaka):** Tange's Festival Plaza space-frame roof vs. Okamoto's Tower of the Sun — the *primitive breaking through the mechanistic*. The Tower of the Sun (70 m, three faces, part building) is the direct ancestor of our wacky-strata landmarks. Isozaki's Festival Plaza robots ("Deme") are the ancestor of our municipal machines.
- **Arata Isozaki:** "Change became constant" — the only style is adaptation. The dust-era city is pure Isozaki: nothing is finished, everything is maintained.

## 5. THE DUST EXTRAPOLATION — element by element

Each entry: **real Showa reference → 40 years of dust → gameplay/art implication.**

### 5.1 The shotengai storefront → the dust arcade
**Reference:** Photo 1's wooden machiya — 4-depth signage sediment (fascia, noren, banners, plates).
**+40 years dust:** The timber facade is sand-blasted to silver-gray; paint survives only in sheltered crevices. The kawara tile roof is half-buried under drifted dust — the shop *digs itself out* each morning (a daily ritual, visible in-game as swept dust piles). The 4-depth signage compresses to 2: fascia boards are gone (wind-torn), but **enamel plates survive and thrive** — sand-blasting keeps them legible. Noren curtains are now heavy canvas dust flaps, weighted at the hem with sewn-in chain links. The menu banners are shorter, stiffer, oilcloth.
**Implication:** drive-up shopfronts only (canon). The swept-dust pile by each door is a cheap decal that sells "people live here" with zero AI. Enamel signage becomes the district's *persistent* information layer — quest-relevant signs are always enamel. Delivery destinations and loading restrictions must remain readable by signs and the paper map, without a GPS overlay. Add legible EMPTY / RESERVED / READY states and input/output icons to existing business boards so a stock change has a visible consequence.

### 5.2 The bonnet bus → the dust hauler
**Reference:** Photo 2's Isuzu-style bonnet bus — truck chassis, exposed hood, round headlights, destination blind.
**+40 years dust:** The exposed hood now carries a **cyclone filter intake** (a conical dust separator, like a miniature cement mixer) — the bus breathes through it. Headlights are caged behind wire guards. The destination blind is replaced by an enamel route plate (permanent — routes don't change in the dust). Side panels carry spare filter canisters like ammunition. The cream/red two-tone is now dust-ochre over rust-red primer.
**Implication:** a traffic archetype (the Hauler) and a potential player vehicle class. The filter canister is a *consumable* — proposed gameplay: filters clog on long dust runs and need a quoted service cost. This is an expression of the setting, not a competitor-exclusivity claim. Better filters slow wear, not an end to service; ordinary jobs retain a viable operating margin and recovery path.

### 5.3 The tram → the armored dust tram
**Reference:** Photo 3's red single-car tram on embedded rails under the wire jungle.
**+40 years dust:** The tram is the street's **icebreaker** — a riveted steel pilot plow on the front, wire-mesh window guards, the clerestory roof sealed with gasketed hatches. The overhead wires are still there but now carry *thicker* feeder cables (the tram is the district's power bus — it drags a power feed). The *chin-chin* bell is replaced by a compressed-air horn (audible through dust). Rails are kept clear by the tram's own passage — the one vehicle that never stops.
**Implication:** retain the Harbor Tram visual/world role; dust-clearing slipstream behavior is a later proposal, not implemented proof or a dependency of the first supply chains. Warn drivers of its route and preserve current traffic work.

### 5.4 The wire jungle → the cable canopy
**Reference:** Photo 3's overhead catenary forest; Miyazaki's "jumbles of wooden utility poles."
**+40 years dust:** Wooden poles are gone (rotted, replaced). The wires remain — but now they're **structural**: pneumatic tubes, power feeds, and guy-wires form a sagging canopy over the street, crusted with dust into a semi-solid roof. The street's "sky" is a 6-meter-high tangle of black cable and brass tube. Light filters through in shafts.
**Implication:** the Lower City's machine-trench look gets its ceiling. Cheap to build (instanced cable splines), massive atmosphere payoff, and it *justifies* the low far-plane: you can't see far because there's a roof of wire.

### 5.5 Giyōfū / foreign-settlement facades → sand-blasted hybrids
**Reference:** Photos 3–4's European-Japanese blend — brick facades, arched windows, verandas on timber frames, Japanese tile roofs on Western bodies.
**+40 years dust:** Sand-blasting strips plaster to expose brick in patches — the facade becomes a **map of its own construction**: brick where the wind hits, plaster where it's sheltered. Arched windows are bricked halfway up (dust-proofing) leaving squinting half-arches. The veranda becomes an enclosed dust-lock (glazed with amber acrylic). Japanese tile roofs survive best — kawara sheds dust by design.
**Implication:** Daikoku/Nagisa's foreign-settlement districts get instant visual identity: half-arch windows + exposed brick patches + amber dust-locks. The "blend" is now legible as *struggle* — two architectures weathering differently.

### 5.6 The policeman → the masked kōban keeper
**Reference:** Photo 4's Showa policeman — dark wool uniform, peaked cap, the koban system's human fixture.
**+40 years dust:** The wool uniform is now waxed canvas; the peaked cap has a **respirator clipped to its brim**; white gloves are pigskin work gloves. The kōban (police box) is a **dust-lock booth** with a pneumatic message tube — the keeper never leaves it during storms. Authority is still a fixture, but a *sealed* one.
**Implication:** feeds the KMTED design (bureaucratic heat). The kōban bribe-chute is now physically motivated: you fire the envelope *through the dust-lock slot*.

### 5.7 Noren → the dust flap (shop identity law)
**Reference:** Photo 1's 御食事 noren; Photo 4's doorway curtains.
**+40 years dust:** Every shop's noren is weighted canvas with the shop's mon stenciled in whitewash. In dust storms they hang *inward*, sucked by pressure differential — the street reads the storm by watching the noren. Different districts use different canvas weaves (a visual dialect).
**Implication:** zero-cost "weather UI" — players read wind/storm state from the curtains. Shop identity survives the dust because the noren is the cheapest replaceable part of the storefront.

### 5.8 The handcart → the trunk economy
**Reference:** Photo 4's daihachiguruma (handcart) in the crowd.
**+40 years dust:** The wheelman does not pull a cart through the dust; suited loading workers at their posts remain legal. Each working *car* has a cart's descendant: the **roof-rack and trunk rig**. Cargo is strapped, tarped, and chained on external racks (visible load = visible gameplay: mass and condition are the first model; independent rigid bodies and spills are later experiments). The handcart's DNA survives as the *manner* of loading: everything is lashed by hand, with rope.
**Implication:** reinforces "the wheelman never leaves the wheel" — loading happens at a marked bay through workers/equipment. A heavy consolidated load saves handling/empty returns but costs acceleration, braking and access; fast repeated runs retain an alley/urgency niche. Keep customer freight separate from owned stock. Garage shelves hold only legally owned materials; painted bay numbers/rental placards show staged growth. Use existing art vocabulary; commission no new images in this reconciliation.
