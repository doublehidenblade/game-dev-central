## Full transcript (translated)

Many people are used to throwing a single prompt at a large model and expecting it to hand-craft a complete 3D game building. The result is usually a pile of useless models you can't even walk through the door of. In reality, when building game scenes with the 3D generation model Tripo 2.0 plus a code model, the smart approach is to completely give up on generating whole buildings in one shot. Treat the entire building like Lego: break the whole village into 22 standard components. Master five practical hard rules, and a large model can precisely assemble an entire interactive water village inside a game engine.

GPT-6 Astra handles the reference images. Tripo 2.0 handles the 3D assets. Claude Opus 5.5 puts everything together in the Godot engine and writes the assembly code. The full scene is 22 Lego-like components that snap together — and all of it was figured out through a pile of mistakes and failures.

**Hard rule one: draw three-views.** Don't rely on text descriptions. The most typical wrong turn in early testing was having the model build an entire wooden cabin and then cut it into pieces. The requirement was to cut the cabin into 6–15 parts; the model spat out 256 fragments in one go — cut so fine nothing was usable. Next they switched to pure text prompts, e.g. "small wooden wall with door." Tripo generated 4 at a time; out of 16 walls tried, only 4 were usable — sizes were pure luck. Some walls had no door at all, some looked like fences. Trying to force Tripo's mesh editor to carve open a sealed doorway failed twice. Text alone doesn't work — what about a floor plan? The model spat out a solid cube, because from the front, a thin wall and a solid cube look identical. The method that actually worked was three-views: draw front, side, and mirror-side on a black background with no clutter, feed them to Tripo 2.0's multi-view mode, and the model finally understood how thick a wall is. The generated parts matched the drawings exactly, the mesh stayed clean quads, polycount freely adjustable. 7 of 8 walls got precisely cut door holes; stilt platforms, four boardwalk segments, stairs, and ladders all came out clean. The first batch of parts for the 22 components finally landed.

**Hard rule two: every component must have real dimensions.** When Opus 5.5 first assembled the components, the boardwalk's wooden stilts were all floating high above the water. The reason was simple: every reference image had a different aspect ratio, so Tripo's 3D dimensions went every which way. But modular components must align with each other. The fix: calibrate all drawings to one grid size — walls 2m wide, 2.4m high, dimensions locked down hard. For the last few centimeters of seam error, Opus 5.5 wrote an alignment script that auto-measures component sizes, corrects deviation, and snaps every corner precisely to the grid. Two walls aligned edge to edge, seams never leaking again.

**Hard rule three: explicitly tell the model what you DON'T want.** Look at this batch of thatched roofs: front and side look perfectly normal, but rotate the 3D model to top view and nearly a third of the roof is holes. In a game, holes in the roof mean leaking light and rain — one sweep of a high camera angle and you see straight through. The fix: when generating the roof reference image, add the key negative requirements for GPT-6 Astra — thatch thick with no gaps, complete when seen from below. The regenerated roofs dropped to near-zero holes. While at it, they added ridge decorations, flags, barrels, fish-drying racks, and a small boat in one pass — all 22 components complete.

**Hard rule four: put a humanoid character in to test the scene.** No matter how refined it looks, if the character can't walk into it, the game is scrap. The village's swamp warrior was also made with Tripo 2.0: draw front/side/back, generate four versions — all solid quality. Tripo's built-in auto-rigging put a skeleton straight onto the model, finger details bound. Hook up a free animation library and walk/run/idle took seconds. But the moment it went into the game for testing, it fell apart: the walk animation's built-in displacement turned into ice-skating in-game, and the long spear on his back bent like a drawn bow with every step — bizarre. Opus 5.5 stepped in: lock animations to in-place playback, let the Godot engine measure actual movement speed to match the stride — skating eliminated — and rigidly fix the spear to the character's back, stable like a real weapon. Set the most basic collision rules in the Godot engine: floors must be walkable, walls impenetrable. Drop in an auto-patrolling little robot: it walked the village from the small boat all the way to the beast skeleton without falling in the water once. But then a new problem: the warrior walked into the village and the doors were too short — he couldn't get in. And this is exactly where the modular component pack shows its power. With a traditional single-piece model, the whole village would have to be rebuilt. Now, just change the cabin's base dimension on the grid from 2m to 2.5m, and every cabin and doorway in the village scales up proportionally in an instant.

**Hard rule five is a hidden detail — invisible at first glance: the whole component pack shares a single texture atlas.** Tripo's generated models come with their own textures, and the built-in smart UV unwrap flattens a 3D model like opening a cardboard box — very convenient per model. But if 22 components each carry their own textures, the scene ends up with 23 independent material sets. A 30cm wooden stake hogs an entire texture — pixels badly wasted — while a 4m roof gets the same-sized texture and goes blurry. And 23 independent materials add overhead to the game engine. The correct industrial practice is a texture atlas: merge all building components by real surface area into the first big atlas, swamp environment and beast skeleton into the second. 23 independent materials compressed to just 2. Every part's texture detail crisp and consistent — sharp where it should be sharp, not a wasted pixel. Even better: because it's all modular components, one keypress seamlessly switches the scene to a 45° isometric view; when the character walks into a house the roof auto-hides; passing the beast skeleton, the bones go translucent — still the same 22 components, not one new model built.

Stop making AI guess an entire house in one breath. Let Tripo 2.0 focus on making clean little components, let the large model write the assembly code in the engine, let GPT-6 Astra own the three-views. The whole village plus character cost a little over 4,000 Tripo credits — 4,160 in total, since every part generated four candidate versions. Drop the obsession with making the large model brute-force an entire building; treat it as a parts workshop on an industrial line. Once dimensions, collision, and textures are all tamed by rules, hand-building an interactive game world solo became reality long ago. Master these building-block rules — what kind of game scene would you most want to build with them?
